AI for Troubleshooting

One tool down. The whole 8D. Run as a loop.

Presented by Andrew Hughes

Welcome. AI Basics covered how these tools work, how to ask well, and how to check an answer. Today we put that to work on the thing that eats our nights and weekends: a tool that keeps going down. Ninety minutes. One real-looking problem, worked through every step of an 8D on your own laptop. The case is the example. The principles are what you take home, and they work on any problem, not just this one.

Monday, 7:00 a.m.

ETCH-04 B is down again. The fourth time since Friday's PM.

A new part went in at the PM. Everyone already knows the cause.

In the next 90 minutes, you'll take it through a full 8D with AI, and make sure it doesn't come back.

Set the scene. You walk in Monday morning. ETCH-04 chamber B is down again, and it's the same alarm every time: He backside flow high. Each reset buys a few hours. (Space.) A new helium MFC went in at Friday's chuck PM, and the night shift is sure that's it. Most of us have been in this room. (Space.) Today you'll work this exact problem yourself, from the first interview to the lesson learned. Don't decide yet whether the night shift is right.

Two ways AI goes wrong in troubleshooting.

A plan that's frivolous, or dangerous

Write me a plan to get 04B running.

Swap the He MFC. If the alarm returns, replace the chuck.

Stuck down one branch

Night shift says it's the new MFC. Can you confirm?

Yes. The alarms start right after the MFC swap…

Parts and downtime spent on a guess, while a simpler answer sits in the manual. Today's workflow is built to stop both.

Two failure modes, and both are expensive. (Space.) One: AI writes a confident plan that swaps parts on a guess, or skips a lockout. It reads well, and it costs parts, downtime, or worse. (Space.) Two: it follows the first idea in the room. Tell it the night shift thinks it's the MFC, and it will find reasons the MFC is the answer, while a simpler check sits in the manual. (Space.) Used well, AI does the opposite: it keeps the search wide so you don't get stuck on one branch. The workflow today is built to get that, and to stop both failures. We'll go through why each step is where it is.

01

Framing the event

Describe it before anyone names a cause. The AI asks the questions. You supply the facts.

Transition, out loud: "Before anyone touches data, we write down what we actually know. This covers D0 to D3 of the 8D, and it's where AI saves the first hour."

Don't write the perfect prompt. Let it interview you.

Ask for the answer

ETCH-04 keeps going down on a helium alarm. What's wrong?

Common causes of He backside alarms include a worn chuck, MFC faults, wafer warpage, particles on the chuck…

Let it ask

It's down on He flow high since the PM. Ask me what you need to know.

What: Clear. Where: Fuzzy, ETCH-05 B not known. When: Unknown, did 04B alarm before the PM?

A gives you a textbook. B gives you a to-do list. (H)

Two ways to start. Ask A is what most of us type at 7 a.m. (Space.) You get a textbook list that would fit any helium alarm on any tool. It's not wrong, it's just not about your tool. (Space.) B is how the playbook starts. You talk it through the way you'd tell a colleague, messy and out of order. The prompt does the structuring: it asks what's missing, marks each part Clear, Fuzzy or Unknown, and stops after four rounds. Nobody thinks in perfect prompts. (Space.) This is H: you supply the facts, the AI asks. Every Unknown it finds is a check you'll need.

D1 and D3 come first

D1 · Team

Who owns each decision

Who owns the record, who runs the checks, who takes the 2 a.m. call

D3 · Containment

What's held, right now

The tool, and every lot that ran on it since the change

AI drafts both. Only you can commit them. (O)

Two disciplines people skip under pressure. D1: who owns this? On a weekend, the answer to "who approves a part swap" decides whether the techs can keep going. D3: the tool is down now, but it ran between every reset. The lots that ran since Friday's PM need a hold until someone checks them. (Space.) The interview asks about both, and the AI drafts them in the record. But a hold or a role isn't real until a person commits to it. That's O: you own what goes out under your name.

The heart of D2: the Kepner-Tregoe Is / Is Not most of you already use. What, where, when, how much. For each, what the problem is, and what it could be but isn't. (Space.) The highlighted cells are the gold. Each UNKNOWN is a check, and the "is not" column is what kills wrong causes later. If it's only 04B and only after the PM, the facility helium can't be it: that line feeds every chamber.

Tell it what's fact, and what's only an idea.

Binding

Has a source

"0.41 sccm at 22 C" on the PM sign-off

Example

Shows a kind of thing

"A reset buys a few hours" becomes a pattern

Background

True, constrains nothing

Crew B did the PM. P-110 ran all weekend.

Guess

Someone's idea

"It's the new MFC." A cause to test, never evidence.

"I believe" pulls hardest. A guess repeated as fact sends everyone down one branch. (O)

Wang et al., "When Truth Is Overridden," 2025

The interview sorts everything you say into four bins. Binding: an observation with a source. Example: something that illustrates a pattern. Background: true, but it doesn't constrain anything. Guess: someone's idea of the cause. The night shift's MFC theory is a guess. It goes on the list of causes to test, and it stays out of the facts. (Space.) This matters more with AI than with people. A 2025 study traced where agreement comes from inside the models: opinions stated in the first person, "I believe", caused more agreement than the same opinion attributed to someone else. So say "the night shift suspects", or hold your own guess back until the data talks. You decide what counts as fact: that's O.

02

Widening with data and documents

The simpler answer is often already written down. Let the data and the manual move the causes.

Transition, out loud: "We have six possible causes. Before anyone touches the tool, let's see what the files already rule out."

These tools remember nothing. The record does.

39%

average drop in performance when a task is spread over many turns

Every cycleNew chat. Paste the latest 8D record. Attach only the new evidence. Nothing else.

Old chats stay closed. Stray details can't leak into the causes.

Laban et al., "LLMs Get Lost in Multi-Turn Conversation," 2025

Long chats drift. A 2025 study across many models found an average 39% drop when the same task was handed over in pieces across many turns instead of all at once. Early wrong turns stick. (Space.) So the playbook never relies on chat memory. One document, the 8D record, carries everything. Each cycle starts a new chat with the latest record and only the new evidence. It's a bit more pasting, and it works in any chat tool. The principle goes well beyond troubleshooting: when the work matters, keep the state in a document you control.

Before a single step is written, the AI shows a short decision menu: which causes this cycle splits, how much downtime, parts, and when the tech stops and calls. Each option comes with pros, cons and what goes wrong if it's the wrong choice. Then it stops and waits. (Space.) Your pick here is the cheap one: split four causes with checks that don't open the chamber. An option that swaps a part before anything is confirmed shouldn't even be on the menu. That's H: the engineer decides the shape; the AI fills it in.

Four rules stop a dangerous plan.

Conditions

On every reading

60 C, 30-minute soak. A cold check is this case's escape.

Confirmation

Before any repair

No swap until a cause is confirmed

Branches

Every result goes somewhere

Next step, stop and call, or the qual

Safety

Cited, never made up

No procedure line, no step

A standard turns a judgment call into a list you can check. (O)

Every step in the plan has to meet four rules. Conditions on every reading: temperature, soak time, setpoint. A leak check without a temperature is exactly how this problem got past sign-off. Confirmation before repair: a fix appears only for a cause that's confirmed and approved. Branches: every result leads to the next step, a stop-and-call, or the qual; nothing dead-ends. Safety: every lockout and limit cites the procedure; where the procedure is silent, the step says NOT IN SOURCE and the tech stops. (Space.) These rules are why a tech can run the plan at 2 a.m. without you. That's O: the plan goes out under your name.
