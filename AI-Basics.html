What AI is: math that learns patterns from examples. How it learns: it adjusts numbers, think of them as knobs, until its predictions match the examples. Here the knobs are a recipe: pressure, RF power and gas flow, predicting film thickness. In the office it could be staffing and scope predicting how long a project takes. Training starts on its own when the slide comes up and runs for about 30 seconds, so talk over it. Each round, the model compares its prediction with the measured value and nudges every knob a little to shrink the gap. It overshoots and corrects, again and again, smaller each time, until after a few hundred rounds the prediction matches the measurement. (Space for the line below.) Nobody sets the knobs by hand; the examples do. We will tell the story in three steps, from a trend line you have already built in Excel to the language models behind today's tools. Same idea every time, just bigger.

The simplest model: a line through a scatter of points. Etch time against etch depth on the floor, or weeks of training against time to qualify in the office. (Space draws the line.) The math picks the slope that misses the points by the least. (Space.) Then it predicts depth for a time it has never seen. This is the core idea behind all AI: learn from examples, then predict new cases. If you have ever added a trendline in Excel, you have already built one. Real models take many inputs at once, one knob for each, such as pressure, power, and gas flow to predict film thickness, or staffing and uptime to forecast a ship date. Same idea.

Some patterns, like a scratch on a wafer image, can't be written as a formula. So the model gets millions of knobs arranged in layers, tuned on thousands of labeled wafer images. (Space.) Early layers pick up simple shapes such as edges and spots. Later layers combine those into patterns, such as a scratch or a particle. Each wrong guess in training nudges the knobs a little. This is what powers AI vision for defect inspection.

The same pattern math that makes AI useful also explains when it goes wrong. Three strengths, each with its matching weak spot. (Space reveals each weak spot.) Broad knowledge, learned from a huge library of text, but it doesn't know our recipes, SOPs, or last night's tool history unless you hand them over. Fluent and fast, and just as fluent when the fit is poor. Helpful, because it was tuned on people's ratings, so it leans toward agreeing with you. (Space.) The current picture: errors are less common when they work from a document you give them, but they have not gone away; even the newest models still make things up in about 7 to 15% of document summaries (Vectara leaderboard, October 2026). Knowing why helps you use it with confidence. The next slides take each one in turn, with a real case.

A confident tone is not a sign of accuracy.

Asked for the max chamber pressure on our recipe, it answers 150 mTorr with no hedge. It has never seen our recipe. (Space.) Why: patterns such as grammar and common physics come out reliably, but a one-off fact it rarely saw, like a part number, a limit, or a date, can't be predicted from patterns. On top of that, the way models have been trained and scored long rated a confident guess higher than "I don't know," like a test with no penalty for guessing (OpenAI, "Why language models hallucinate," September 2025). Newer models hold back more often, which cuts these errors, but not to zero (OpenAI's own tests). (Space.) The real case: in February 2023, a promotional demo ad for Google's Bard chatbot said the James Webb telescope took the first picture of a planet outside our solar system. It didn't. Alphabet's shares closed about 8% down that day, roughly $100 billion in market value (NPR, Reuters). One fluent, confident, wrong sentence that nobody checked. (Space.) A confident tone is not a sign of accuracy. These errors cluster on specific numbers and facts about our own tools, which is exactly where section 02 starts.

Like recalling a part number from memory: the format is perfect, but the number may not be. The edge moves out every time you hand it a source.

The best evidence on knowledge work: Harvard and Boston Consulting Group ran an experiment with 758 consultants. The picture: AI's ability isn't a smooth line. Tasks that look equally hard land on either side of a jagged edge. (Space.) Inside the edge, people with AI finished 12% more tasks, 25% faster, at more than 40% higher quality. Lean on it for drafting, explaining, summarizing what you give it, and brainstorming. It's faster than any of us at these. (Space.) On one task chosen to sit just outside the edge, people using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to get the right answer than people without it. They trusted a fluent wrong answer. For us, outside the edge means our recipes and limits, current tool state, and anything you'd cite. (Space.) Here's the part people miss: the edge isn't fixed. Every time you hand it a source, the edge moves out. That's exactly what section 02 teaches.

One picture for everything in this section. The language model is an engine: all power, no direction. It can run at full speed and go nowhere. That's the chat on its own: fluent, fast, and guessing about our facts. (Space.) Steering and brakes: the rules and the sources. What keeps it on the road is the document you hand it, the limits you set, and the checks you run. In a chat, you supply these. (Space.) Wheels and sensors: the tools that touch the road, the parts that reach real data and real systems. A plain chat has few of them, which is why it can't see our recipes or last night's tool history. More of these get built into the tools you already use over time. (Space.) And a driver: you. Say it plainly and move on: the next two sections are how you steer and brake.

Illustration
The quick ask gets a generic list, because the model has to guess what you want. Add the four parts one at a time. (Space) Context: what's going on, and the document it should use. PM is preventive maintenance. (Space) Audience: who will read the answer. (Space) Constraints: what to stick to, and what to stay away from. (Space) Format: the shape you want back. Watch the answer change. Why Audience and not "You are an expert": research (Zheng et al. 2024; Wharton, December 2025) finds expert personas don't make answers more accurate, while saying who will read the answer does shape its tone and level. Not every question needs all four: a quick question can be one line. Use all four for answers you'll use or send. Thirty seconds of setup saves about ten minutes of back and forth (a rule of thumb, not a measured result). The same four parts work for an office ask, such as explaining a budget variance. AI output that looks finished but pushes the real work onto the reader.
