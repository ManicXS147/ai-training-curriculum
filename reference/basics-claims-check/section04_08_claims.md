# AI Basics speaker notes, "What workslop is" to end: claims check

Scope: speaker notes from "What workslop is" through "Try one thing today". Internal course references (AI for Leaders, prompts.txt, practice pack, section cross-references) and facilitation instructions cannot be checked and are not listed. Verdict scale: Supported / Partly supported (with correction) / Unsupported / Outdated.

## Does each number and attribution match the source exactly?

### Takeaway
The workslop numbers (1,150 US desk workers, 40%, nearly two hours, about half) and the Schilke and Reimann citation match their sources. Two claims need fixing. The Samsung ban was a temporary ban in one division, not a company-wide ban. The "a little" in the disclosure line does not come from the paper. BetterUp's own blog also gives a different sample size, so the deck should keep the figure the HBR authors and Axios used.

### Cited Findings

**Claim 1: "What workslop is": definition, and the idea that thinking moves from sender to reader.** Verdict: Supported (the definition). The HBR piece defines workslop as "AI generated work content that masquerades as good work, but lacks the substance to meaningfully advance a given task." The research is by BetterUp Labs with the Stanford Social Media Lab, and HBR published it on 23 September 2025. Authors: Niederhoffer, Kellerman, Lee, Liebscher, Rapuano, Hancock — [beSpacific excerpt of HBR](https://www.bespacific.com/?p=117230); [HBR](https://hbr.org/2025/09/ai-generated-workslop-is-destroying-productivity)
- "Thinking moves from the sender to the reader" matches the article's main argument that the burden shifts to the recipient. I could only open the summary and introduction of the HBR page (it is paywalled), so I could not check this exact wording against the full text. Treat it as a paraphrase, not a quote.

**Claim 2: "BetterUp Labs and the Stanford Social Media Lab, 2025, survey of 1,150 US desk workers."** Verdict: Supported, with one note.
- Axios: researchers "in August and September" polled "1,150 U.S. adults who described themselves as desk workers" — [Axios, 24 Sep 2025](https://www.axios.com/2025/09/24/ai-workslop-workplace-efficiency-study)
- BetterUp's infographic footnote says "1,150 full-time U.S. desk workers". BetterUp's own blog text says "September 2025 survey of 1,004 full-time U.S. desk workers", so BetterUp's own materials disagree — [BetterUp blog, 29 Sep 2025](https://www.betterup.com/blog/hidden-costs-workslop)
- Suggested wording: "a 2025 survey of about 1,150 full-time US desk workers."

**Claim 3: "40% received workslop in the past month."** Verdict: Supported.
- "40% of employees believe they've received workslop in the last month." Managers reported 54% and individual contributors 38.5% — [BetterUp blog](https://www.betterup.com/blog/hidden-costs-workslop)
- "40% said they'd encountered this stuff in the last month" — [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2025/09/24/ai-workslop-workplace-efficiency-study)
- The HBR summary page I could open says "41% of workers have encountered such AI-generated output" and gives no time frame — [HBR](https://hbr.org/2025/09/ai-generated-workslop-is-destroying-productivity). This is probably rounding of the same figure. 40% is the figure tied to "last month".
- Precision note: respondents *believe* they received workslop. This is self-reported.

**Claim 4: "Each one took nearly two hours to deal with."** Verdict: Supported.
- Axios gives "an average of 1 hour and 56 minutes dealing with each instance" — [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2025/09/24/ai-workslop-workplace-efficiency-study)
- BetterUp's blog gives "1 hour and 51 minutes on average". The infographic rounds it to 2 hours — [BetterUp blog](https://www.betterup.com/blog/hidden-costs-workslop)
- HBR says "nearly two hours of rework per instance" — [HBR](https://hbr.org/2025/09/ai-generated-workslop-is-destroying-productivity). "Nearly two hours" holds under every version, and it is an average.

**Claim 5: "About half saw the sender as less capable, creative, and reliable afterward."** Verdict: Supported.
- "About half viewed the sender as less creative, capable, and reliable." In the same survey, 42% saw the sender as less trustworthy, over a third as less intelligent, and nearly one in three were less likely to want to work with that person again — [BetterUp blog](https://www.betterup.com/blog/hidden-costs-workslop); same wording in [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2025/09/24/ai-workslop-workplace-efficiency-study)

**Claim 6: "The flip side: clear, checked work builds that same trust."** Verdict: Unsupported as a research finding. The survey measured how workslop hurts perceptions of the sender. It did not test whether checked work builds trust. None of the sources I opened measured this.
- Suggested wording: "The flip side is the habit we're about to practice: send work the reader can act on."

**Claim 7: Schilke and Reimann, 2025, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 13 experiments: disclosing AI use lowers trust "a little", and "being found out by someone else lowers it further".** Verdict: Partly supported.
- Citation confirmed: Schilke, O., & Reimann, M. (2025). "The transparency dilemma: How AI disclosure erodes trust." *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 188, 104405. doi:10.1016/j.obhdp.2025.104405 — [RePEc/IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v188y2025ics0749597825000172.html)
- 13 experiments: confirmed. Main finding: "actors who disclose their AI usage are trusted less than those who do not" — [RePEc/IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v188y2025ics0749597825000172.html)
- "Being found out lowers it further": supported. The disclosure penalty is "comparatively weaker than the effect of third-party exposure" (Studies 9–13). The penalty also held whether disclosure was voluntary or mandatory and across different disclosure framings — [RePEc/IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v188y2025ics0749597825000172.html). The paper's term is "third-party exposure"; "found out by someone else" is a fair plain-language version.
- "A little": not in the paper. The abstract presents the disclosure penalty as robust across 13 studies and only says it is *weaker than exposure*. A within-paper meta-analysis found the penalty smaller, but still present, among evaluators with favorable attitudes toward technology — [RePEc/IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v188y2025ics0749597825000172.html). Calling it "a little" understates it.
- "Saying what you checked is what keeps the trust": Unsupported by this paper. The abstract reports that the penalty held across the disclosure framings tested, and I found no study showing that adding "what I checked" removes the penalty. This is the trainer's advice and should be presented that way.
- Suggested wording: "A 2025 study across 13 experiments (Schilke and Reimann, *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*) found that people who disclose AI use are trusted less, but being exposed by someone else costs more trust than disclosing it yourself. Our advice is to pair the disclosure with what you checked, so the reader knows a person stands behind it."

**Claim 8: Samsung, spring 2023. Within weeks of allowing a public chatbot, engineers had pasted confidential source code and meeting notes into it.** Verdict: Supported. Add "reportedly", because Samsung did not confirm the details.
- Korean media (economist.co.kr) reported at least three incidents less than three weeks after Samsung lifted its ChatGPT ban on 11 March 2023, in the Device Solutions / semiconductor unit. The incidents were: source code for a semiconductor database download program pasted in for troubleshooting, program code for identifying defective equipment, and recorded meeting notes uploaded to generate minutes — [The Register, 6 Apr 2023](https://www.theregister.com/2023/04/06/samsung_reportedly_leaked_its_own)
- Samsung "didn't confirm or deny" the reports — [The Register, 2 May 2023](https://www.theregister.com/2023/05/02/samsung_generative_ai_ban/)

**Claim 9: "Samsung banned generative AI tools on company devices in May 2023."** Verdict: Partly supported. The ban was temporary and limited to one division.
- Korea Economic Daily: the ban applied to the Device eXperience (DX) division (smartphones and appliances). It covered ChatGPT, Bard and Bing on workplace desktops, tablets and phones. The memo was dated 28 April 2023, and Samsung called it "temporarily restricting the use of generative AI". The Device Solutions (semiconductor) division was restricted but not fully banned — [KED Global, 2 May 2023](http://www.kedglobal.com/newsAmp/ked202305020022)
- Fortune, citing Bloomberg: the memo went out Monday 1 May 2023, and a revised memo said the ban was temporary — [Fortune, 2 May 2023](https://fortune.com/2023/05/02/samsung-bans-employee-use-chatgpt-data-leak/). The Register: the memo went to one of Samsung's "biggest divisions" and the ban was "temporary" — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2023/05/02/samsung_generative_ai_ban/)
- Suggested wording: "Samsung then temporarily banned generative AI tools on work devices in a major division (announced around 1 May 2023)."
- Note: the trigger described in the deck (source code from the chip unit) and the division that was banned (DX) are different units. "Samsung found engineers…" plus "Samsung banned…" is still accurate at company level.

### Inferences
- The workslop figures are safe to present as they are. Rounding to "about 1,150" protects against BetterUp's 1,004 vs 1,150 inconsistency.
- The two lines that most need changing are "a little" (Schilke and Reimann) and the unqualified "banned ... on company devices" (Samsung).

### Gaps
- I could not read the full HBR text (paywall). I confirmed the 1,150 sample and the per-month framing through Axios and BetterUp, not HBR's own body text.
- I did not open the Bloomberg Samsung article directly. Its details reached me through Fortune.
- I could not see the Schilke and Reimann effect sizes, so I cannot say how large the disclosure penalty is in numbers.

## Is any claim stated more strongly than the source supports?

### Takeaway
Yes. Four lines go further than their sources: "a little" and "saying what you checked keeps the trust" (Schilke and Reimann), "the good ones show up in the later turns" (brainstorming), and "it checks against the standard you gave it, not against what reports usually look like" (AI review).

### Cited Findings

**Claim 10: "A conversation, not a search … the good ones show up in the later turns."** Verdict: Partly supported (overstated).
- Luan, Kim and Zhou (2025, *Information Systems Research*), 3 studies, about 160 participants each. Across 10 rounds, human-AI pairs "failed to achieve augmented learning spontaneously". Creativity improved over rounds "only when we introduced a deliberate intervention" (instructing people to co-develop ideas). "Idea generation itself had no relationship with joint creativity" — [Cambridge Judge Business School summary](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/2025/how-human-ai-interaction-becomes-more-creative/)
- This supports the deck's *method* (push, combine, narrow, develop). It contradicts the idea that later turns are better *by default*.
- Meincke, Mollick and Terwiesch (2024, Wharton working paper): chain-of-thought prompting "leads to the highest diversity of ideas of all prompts we evaluated", close to the diversity of human groups — [Wharton Mack Institute](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/2024/new-paper-on-prompting-diverse-ideas-increasing-ai-idea-variance/). This supports "ask for more, and say how they should differ."
- Doshi and Hauser (2024, *Science Advances*), 300 writers and 600 raters: AI ideas raised individual novelty and usefulness, especially for less creative writers. AI-assisted stories were more similar to each other (about 10.7% more similar in the one-idea condition) — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/07/240712222127.htm). This supports "expect some generic ideas" in turn one.
- Suggested wording: "The first answer is a starting point. The better ideas come when you push it: ask for different ones, combine them, and develop them with your constraints. Just asking more turns doesn't do it on its own."

**Claim 11: "Refine your own idea: ask whether it's good and it says yes."** Verdict: Supported.
- Sharma et al. (Anthropic), "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models", section 3.1: "AI assistants frequently provide feedback that is more positive when the user states they like or wrote the passage of text." This was tested on arguments, poems and math solutions across five assistants — [arXiv 2310.13548](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.13548v4)
- "Ask for three weaknesses and a skeptic's question" is reasonable advice. I found no study that tests this exact prompt. Present it as a technique.

**Claim 12: AI-drafted documents: "you bring the facts, it brings the structure; anything new is something to check"; "a drafted SOP is a draft until it goes through document control."** Verdict: Sound advice, no specific study needed. This is policy and process guidance, not an empirical claim. No change suggested.

**Claim 13: "Fix this" gets a rewrite in its voice and "can slip in a step you never wrote."** Verdict: Unsupported by a specific source I opened, but plausible. It follows from the deck's own section 03 on hallucination. It is safe as stated ("can").

**Claim 14: "Ask it to be the reader … it's fast at finding gaps."** Verdict: Partly supported.
- Liang et al. (arXiv 2023, later published in NEJM AI) studied GPT-4 feedback on 3,096 Nature-family papers and 1,709 ICLR papers. Its comments overlapped with human reviewers' by 30.85% and 39.23%, about the same as two human reviewers overlap with each other (28.58% and 35.25%). Of 308 researchers, 57.4% found the feedback helpful or very helpful — [arXiv 2310.01783](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01783v1)
- This supports "useful first review, you decide which matter". It does not show the AI catches everything. The deck's "a first review" framing is appropriately modest.

**Claim 15: "Check it against the standard … It checks against the standard you gave it, not against what reports usually look like."** Verdict: Overstated, and I found no source. Giving the source document does ground the model, but models can still mix in general expectations. Suggested wording: "It checks mainly against the standard you gave it, so give it the real one."

**Claim 16: Disclosure line and "Saying what you checked is what keeps the trust."** Covered under Claim 7. Present it as advice, not a research finding.

### Inferences
- The brainstorming slide is the main place where a line implies evidence that the research does not back. The 2025 ISR study says almost the opposite unless people are deliberately steered. The deck's five follow-up moves are the kind of deliberate steering the study says works.

### Gaps
- I did not open the full Doshi and Hauser paper (the PMC page returned a captcha). Details come from the ScienceDaily release.
- I did not verify the NEJM AI publication of Liang et al. directly. I opened only the arXiv version.

## For advice with no cited source, is there credible research?

### Takeaway
Most of the no-source advice in these sections is process guidance that needs no citation: document control, strip names before pasting, would-I-sign-this. Where the notes imply evidence, the research is mixed. Iterating helps only when it is deliberate (Luan et al. 2025), asking for weaknesses counters sycophancy (Sharma et al.), and AI review is useful but partial (Liang et al.).

### Cited Findings
- Deliberate co-development, not more turns alone, drives better human-AI ideas — [Cambridge Judge](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/2025/how-human-ai-interaction-becomes-more-creative/)
- Prompting for differences (chain-of-thought) increases AI idea diversity — [Wharton Mack Institute](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/2024/new-paper-on-prompting-diverse-ideas-increasing-ai-idea-variance/)
- Models give more positive feedback on text the user says they wrote or like — [arXiv 2310.13548](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.13548v4)
- GPT-4 review comments overlap with human reviewers about as much as humans overlap with each other — [arXiv 2310.01783](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01783v1)

### Inferences
- These need no source: "each habit takes seconds", "most of these checks take seconds", "you never have to pick between safe and fast", and "did the answer lose anything when names came out? Most will find it's the same answer". They are facilitation claims, not research claims. The last one is an untested prediction. A safer phrasing: "Usually you'll find you get the same help."
- "What goes into a public tool can't be called back" is true in practice for 2023 consumer ChatGPT. Default data handling varies by tool and by enterprise contract (Copilot Chat with enterprise data protection differs). The line is fine as a reason for the habit.

### Gaps
- I found no study on the four "hand it back" questions or on whether "ask everyone every time" reduces conflict. Present these as course practice.
- I found no study on summarizing the chat and restarting when a long chat drifts. This is a known context-length issue, but I did not source it here.
