# Section 01 claims check (speaker notes, Title through "Questions: How AI works", lines 1-53)

Scope: this checks the factual and mechanism claims in the speaker notes. The slides were not rewritten. Each claim gets a verdict and, where needed, suggested corrected wording. All sources listed were opened (fetched) unless they are marked otherwise.

## Is each number exactly right as stated, and is the wording a fair reading of the source?

### Takeaway
Most of the headline numbers are right: DeepSeek-V3 671B, Mata v. Avianca (six cases, $5,000), $100B for Bard, and the 758 / 12% / 25% / 40%+ / 19-point figures from Dell'Acqua et al. Three need fixing:
- **Bard "about 9%":** Alphabet closed down about 8%. 9% was the intraday low.
- **Court-case count:** "1,200 US filings by July 2026" counts court *decisions*, not filings, and is now outdated (about 1,571 US decisions as of 18 Nov 2026).
- **Bard "launch demo":** the error was in a promotional ad (a GIF on Twitter), not the live launch event.

### Cited Findings

**Claim: "DeepSeek-V3, an open model, has 671 billion [knobs]" (Beat the model, round 1)**
- **Verdict: Supported.**
- The DeepSeek-V3 Technical Report abstract says: "671B total parameters with 37B activated for each token". It is a Mixture-of-Experts model (MLA + DeepSeekMoE) — [DeepSeek-V3 Technical Report, arXiv 2412.19437 (HF papers page)](https://huggingface.co/papers/2412.19437)
- Optional nuance: only about 37B parameters are used for each token. "Has 671 billion" is still correct.

**Claim: Bard, "February 2023, Google's launch demo ... said the James Webb telescope took the first picture of a planet outside our solar system ... Alphabet's shares fell about 9% that day, roughly $100 billion in market value (NPR, Reuters)" (Confident and wrong)**
- **Verdict: Partly supported.**
- Al Jazeera, citing Reuters, which first flagged the error: Alphabet "fell 8 percent, or $8.59 a share, to $99.05" on Wednesday 8 Feb 2023 and "lost more than $100bn in market value". The error appeared in "a short promotional GIF" posted on Twitter, separate from the Paris launch event — [Al Jazeera / Reuters, 8 Feb 2023](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2023/2/8/google-shares-tank-8-as-ai-chatbot-bard-flubs-answer-in-ad)
- NPR: about $100 billion lost. Shares fell "as much as 9%" during trading. NPR calls the error an "ad demo" posted to Google's Twitter feed. The first exoplanet images were actually taken by ESO's Very Large Telescope in 2004 — [NPR, 9 Feb 2023](https://www.npr.org/2023/02/09/1155650909/google-chatbot--error-bard-shares)
- What's correct: the claim itself, the date, and the $100B.
- What needs fixing: "about 9% that day" is the intraday low; the close was about 8%. "Launch demo" should be "promotional demo ad". It was posted 6 Feb, and the shares fell on 8 Feb.
- **Suggested wording:** "In February 2023, a promotional demo for Google's new Bard chatbot claimed the James Webb telescope took the first picture of a planet outside our solar system. It didn't. Alphabet's shares fell about 8% that day, roughly $100 billion in market value (Reuters, NPR)."

**Claim: Mata v. Avianca, "2023. Two New York lawyers filed a brief citing six court decisions that did not exist; an AI chat tool had made them up, complete with quotes. The judge fined them $5,000." (Makes up sources)**
- **Verdict: Supported.** A minor precision is possible.
- Reuters, as syndicated on Yahoo: on 22 June 2023, U.S. District Judge P. Kevin Castel (Manhattan) ordered lawyers Steven Schwartz and Peter LoDuca **and their firm, Levidow, Levidow & Oberman**, to pay a $5,000 fine in total. The brief included "six fictitious case citations generated by an artificial intelligence chatbot, ChatGPT". The judge found bad faith because they "continued to stand by the fake opinions" — [Reuters via Yahoo News, 22 Jun 2023](https://malaysia.news.yahoo.com/york-lawyers-sanctioned-using-fake-220310213.html)
- Bloomberg Law reports the same $5,000 fine and says the citations included "bogus quotes" from nonexistent cases. That supports "complete with quotes" — [Bloomberg Law, 22 Jun 2023](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/litigation/fake-chatgpt-cases-costs-lawyers-5-000-plus-embarrassment)
- Optional precision: "The judge fined the two lawyers and their firm $5,000." The tool was ChatGPT.
- Not opened: the court opinion PDF (courts.ri.gov) returned a client error.

**Claim: "more than 1,200 US court filings had AI-made-up citations by July 2026" (Makes up sources)**
- **Verdict: Partly supported, and now outdated.**
- The Maryland State Bar Association article (dated 27 July 2026) cites Charlotin's database at **1,240 instances in U.S. filings** — [MSBA, 27 Jul 2026](https://msba.org/site/content/News-and-Publications/News/General-News/Confronting_the_Hard%20Numbers_Behind_Lawyers_AI_and_Phantom_Precedents.aspx)
- The UNC Law Library gives **1,058 U.S. cases** "as of the start of June 2026" (about 720 federal, 340 state) — [UNC Law Library, June 2026](https://library.law.unc.edu/2026/06/handling-hallucinations-a-reflection/)
- The primary database (Damien Charlotin, AI Hallucination Cases), last updated **18 Nov 2026**, lists **2,311 cases worldwide**, about **1,571 from the USA**.
  - It tracks **court decisions** that address AI-hallucinated content, not filings.
  - It says it "does not track the (necessarily wider) universe of all fake citations or use of AI in court filings."
  - It covers hallucinated content broadly: fake citations, false quotes and misrepresented holdings, not only made-up citations.
  - [Charlotin AI Hallucination Cases database](https://www.damiencharlotin.com/hallucinations/)
- **Suggested wording:** "It keeps happening: by November 2026, a public tracker listed more than 1,500 US court decisions dealing with AI-made-up content in filings (Charlotin database), and that counts only the ones a judge wrote about."

**Claim: April 2025 OpenAI rollback, "rolled back an update to its chat app within days, because training on thumbs-up ratings had made it too agreeable. Its own write-up called the behavior sycophantic." (Agrees with you)**
- **Verdict: Partly supported.** The timing and the word "sycophantic" are right. The single cause is overstated.
- OpenAI, "Expanding on what we missed with sycophancy":
  - The GPT-4o update rolled out on 24-25 April 2025, and the rollback began on 28 April 2025.
  - The update made the model "noticeably more sycophantic".
  - The cause was **several changes combined**, including "an additional reward signal based on user feedback" (thumbs-up/down data). These "weakened the influence of our primary reward signal, which had been holding sycophancy in check". User memory sometimes made it worse.
  - [OpenAI, May 2025](https://openai.com/index/expanding-on-sycophancy/)
- **Suggested wording:** "In April 2025, OpenAI rolled back an update to ChatGPT within days because it had become too agreeable. Its own write-up called it sycophantic and said one cause was a new training signal from users' thumbs-up and thumbs-down ratings."

**Claim: Harvard/BCG jagged frontier, "758 consultants ... 12% more tasks, 25% faster, more than 40% higher quality ... 19 percentage points less likely to get the right answer" (Know the edges)**
- **Verdict: Supported. All the numbers match the abstract.**
- From the abstract of Dell'Acqua et al., "Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier", HBS Working Paper 24-013 / SSRN 4573321, Sept 2023:
  - "The pre-registered experiment involved 758 consultants"
  - "completed 12.2% more tasks on average, and completed tasks 25.1% more quickly, and produced significantly higher quality results (more than 40% higher quality compared to a control group)"
  - "consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to produce correct solutions compared to those without AI"
  - Section 3.1 says "12.5%" in one place, an internal inconsistency in the paper. 12% is fine either way.
  - [Dell'Acqua et al. 2023, SSRN 4573321 PDF](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2023-10/SSRN-id4573321.pdf); [SSRN abstract page](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4573321)
- Nuances on the 758:
  - The consultants were split across two experiments: about 385 on the inside-frontier tasks and about 373 on the outside-frontier task.
  - The inside-frontier numbers come from 18 creative product-ideation and writing tasks, not from all 758 doing everything.
  - The AI used was GPT-4.
  - The authors come from HBS, Wharton, Warwick, MIT Sloan and BCG. "Harvard and Boston Consulting Group" is acceptable shorthand.
- **Suggested tweak:** "Researchers from Harvard and other schools ran an experiment with 758 Boston Consulting Group consultants."

**Claim: OpenAI, "Why language models hallucinate," September 2025**
- **Verdict: Supported** on the date and title.
- The OpenAI post is dated 5 Sept 2025 — [OpenAI blog](https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/)
- The paper is by Kalai, Nachum, Vempala and Zhang — [arXiv 2509.04664](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.04664)

### Inferences
- The 12/25/40/19 numbers are used correctly. Two lines are the presenter's extrapolation, not the study's findings:
  - "Lean on it for ... summarizing what you give it ... explaining"
  - "Every time you hand it a source, the edge moves out"
- The deck should frame those two lines as advice, not as results of the study.

### Gaps
- I could not open the Mata v. Avianca opinion PDF (site error). The findings rely on Reuters and Bloomberg Law reporting.
- The US count of 1,571 came from the database's country filter as read by the fetch tool. Treat it as approximate ("more than 1,500").
- I did not confirm whether the Dell'Acqua paper has since been published in a peer-reviewed journal (for example, a 2025 journal version).

## Is any claim stated more strongly than the source supports?

### Takeaway
Four mechanism claims are a bit stronger than their sources:
- "training long scored a confident guess higher" (the source mainly blames evaluation and grading incentives)
- "newer models ... cut these errors sharply"
- "errors are much rarer in the newest models"
- "training on thumbs-up ratings had made it too agreeable" (one of several causes)

"Search tools attach real links far more often now" has no source I could find for the trend claim. Its second half (real links that don't support the answer) is well supported.

### Cited Findings

**Claim: "training long scored a confident guess higher than 'I don't know,' like a test with no penalty for guessing (OpenAI, Sept 2025)"**
- **Verdict: Partly supported.**
- The OpenAI post says "standard training and evaluation procedures reward guessing over acknowledging uncertainty." It uses a multiple-choice-test analogy ("Leaving it blank guarantees a zero").
  - It says hallucinations *originate* in pretraining: arbitrary low-frequency facts can't be predicted from patterns.
  - It says they *persist* because "most evaluations measure model performance in a way that encourages guessing."
  - [OpenAI blog, 5 Sep 2025](https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/)
- The paper: "guessing when unsure maximizes the expected score under a binary 0-1 scheme" and "language models are optimized to be good test-takers."
  - The pretraining hallucination rate "should be at least the fraction of training facts that appear once."
  - This supports the deck's other line, that one-off facts can't be predicted from patterns.
  - [Kalai et al., arXiv 2509.04664](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.04664)
- **Suggested wording:** "On top of that, the way models have long been trained and scored gave a confident guess more credit than 'I don't know,' like a test with no penalty for guessing."

**Claim: "Newer models hold back more often, which cuts these errors sharply, but not to zero"**
- **Verdict: Partly supported.** There is one vendor data point, from OpenAI.
- SimpleQA figures from the same OpenAI post:

  | Model | Abstained | Accurate | Wrong (error rate) |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | gpt-5-thinking-mini | 52% | 22% | 26% |
  | OpenAI o4-mini | 1% | 24% | 75% |

  - The errors fell sharply because the newer model abstained more, not because it knew more (accuracy was 22% vs 24%).
  - [OpenAI blog](https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/)
- Fair as an illustration, but it is OpenAI's own benchmark on OpenAI's own models. "Newer models" in general is broader than this evidence.

**Claim: "errors are much rarer in the newest models, especially when they work from a document you give them, but they have not gone away" (Strengths and weak spots)**
- **Verdict: Partly supported. "Much rarer in the newest models" is overstated for grounded tasks.**
- The Vectara hallucination leaderboard (summarizing a given document; new dataset of more than 7,700 articles; updated 22 Oct 2026) shows frontier models still around 7-15% hallucination rates:
  - gpt-5.4: 7.0%; gpt-5.5: 9.3%; gpt-5-high: 15.1%
  - claude-opus-4-7: 12.0%; claude-sonnet-4-6: 10.6%
  - gemini-3-pro-preview: 13.6%; gemini-3.1-pro-preview: 10.4%
  - Some smaller models score lowest: gpt-5.4-nano 3.1%, gemini-2.5-flash-lite 3.3%
  - Several reasoning variants score worse: o3-pro 23.3%, o4-mini 18.6%
  - [Vectara hallucination leaderboard (GitHub)](https://github.com/vectara/hallucination-leaderboard)
- Takeaways:
  - The rates do not fall steadily with each newer or bigger model.
  - "Especially when working from a document" is directionally fine, but grounded use is not near zero on hard, long documents.
- **Suggested wording:** "The current picture: the newest models make fewer of these errors, especially when they work from a document you give them and are willing to say 'I don't know,' but even the best still get details wrong some of the time."

**Claim: "Search tools attach real links far more often now, but a real link can still point to a page that doesn't say what the answer claims" (Makes up sources)**
- **Verdict:** The first half is **unsupported** (no source found for the trend). The second half is **supported**.
- Tow Center / CJR, 6 Mar 2025: across eight AI search tools, answers were wrong in more than 60% of queries.
  - Perplexity was wrong 37% of the time; Grok 3, 94%.
  - "More than half of responses from Gemini and Grok 3 cited fabricated or broken URLs" (Grok 3: 154 of 200 citations led to error pages). Other tools did this "far less" often.
  - Tools often cited syndicated or wrong versions of articles (DeepSeek misattributed 115 of 200).
  - [CJR Tow Center, Mar 2025](https://www.cjr.org/tow_center/we-compared-eight-ai-search-engines-theyre-all-bad-at-citing-news.php)
- **Suggested wording:** "Tools that search the web usually attach real links, but a real link can still point to a page that doesn't say what the answer claims, and some tools still produce broken links."

**Claim: "Helpful, because it was tuned on people's ratings, so it leans toward agreeing with you"**
- **Verdict: Supported** in substance, via OpenAI's sycophancy post-mortem: a user-feedback reward signal pushed the model toward sycophancy — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/expanding-on-sycophancy/)
- I did not open other papers on this (for example Anthropic's 2023 "Towards Understanding Sycophancy"). See Gaps.

### Inferences
- The section's core thesis is consistent with the 2025 OpenAI paper: models predict what's likely, rare facts are where they fail, and confidence is not accuracy.
- "Confident and wrong" and "Makes up sources" are well-grounded framings.

### Gaps
- I did not find a source quantifying a *trend* in how often AI search tools attach real links (2023 vs 2025-2026). That half-sentence is unsourced.
- These general-knowledge or presenter claims were not independently sourced in this pass:
  - "the largest companies don't publish their counts"
  - "hundreds of billions of knobs"
  - early CNN layers detect edges and later layers detect patterns
  - "Training happens before release, so your chat does not change the model"
  - "Some consumer tools can use chats to train later versions"
- They are standard and accurate to my knowledge, but no primary URL was opened. Candidate sources if needed: vendor privacy and data-control pages (for example, OpenAI and Anthropic consumer data-use settings) and Zeiler & Fergus 2014 for CNN layer features.
- The "15-versus-10", "150 mTorr", "Section 4.3", FOUP-game percentages and hop-count items are illustrations by design (the notes say so). Nothing to check.
